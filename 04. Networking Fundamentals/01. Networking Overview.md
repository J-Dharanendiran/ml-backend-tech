## 🎮 The Shot That Travelled

You are playing an online game. You shoot another player. Your screen shows the hit, but your computer is not the one deciding what happens next.

The action has to leave your machine, reach a **game server**, and then travel on to the other player's computer **[L]**. On the way it passes through switches, routers and firewalls, reaches the Internet, crosses other networks, and finally arrives at the destination server. The server processes it and sends the relevant data back the same way **[L]**.

> 💭 *"What is networking, really?"*

Networking is **connecting devices so they can communicate and exchange data** **[L]**. The Internet lets computers in different geographical locations talk by passing data across **interconnected networks** **[L]**.

The same idea sits behind everything you do online:

- 🌍 opening a website
- 🔌 calling an API
- 🤖 sending a request to an AI application

*(The last three examples come from your own notes.)*

Now let's build that Internet from nothing, one device at a time.

## 🔌 Two Computers and One Cable

Networking began with a simple need: **computers wanted to share information** **[L]**.

The easiest version is two computers joined by a single **Ethernet cable**. They can exchange data directly **[L]**.

> 💭 *"What happens when I have ten computers? A hundred?"*

Connecting every computer to every other computer one by one quickly becomes impractical **[L]**. The cables would multiply faster than the machines.

### 🔀 Enter the Switch

A **switch** connects many devices (computers, printers) inside a local network so they can all talk to one another **[L]**. Computers plugged into the same switch form a **local network** **[L]**.

| | |
|---|---|
| **What it is** | A device that connects multiple devices inside one local network [L] |
| **Why it exists** | Direct cable-to-cable connections stop scaling [L] |
| **How it works (extra detail, not in the lecture)** | A switch keeps a table of which MAC addresses it has seen on which port [S1], and forwards Ethernet frames by destination MAC [S10] |
| **Analogy** | A coordinator in a room who knows exactly who sits where *(this is a teaching analogy, not a sourced fact)* |

The Cisco-flavoured detail (MAC table, Layer 2) is not in your lecture notes. It is here because you are heading into CCNA, where it will come back.

## 🧭 The Moment a Switch Isn't Enough

Picture two separate networks. Each has several computers, each wired through its own switch.

A computer in the first network wants to reach a computer in the second.

> 🧠 **Pause and predict:** the switch connects everything *it* is plugged into. What is missing for the two networks to reach each other?

Your notes answer it directly: a switch alone does not give you the routing needed to connect separate networks **[L]**.

### 🛣️ Enter the Router

A **router forwards packets between different networks** **[L]**. It is the device sitting between two worlds, deciding where each packet goes next.

Cisco-oriented material draws the clean line like this:

> A router forwards **IP packets between networks**. A switch moves **Ethernet frames inside one network** **[S2]**.

Routing decisions rely on a **routing table**, a record of the paths packets should take **[S6]**. *(I read the Cloudflare routing page through a copy. See the notes at the end.)*

Now the story gets interesting. Connect a router to another router, and then to another, and networks can communicate across large geographical distances **[L]**. That is the principle the Internet is built on **[L]**.

```
   [PC]──[PC]                     [PC]──[PC]
      \  /                           \  /
    [Switch]──────[Router]───────[Router]──────[Switch]
     Network A                                  Network B
```

## 🛡️ Why Not Let Everything In?

Connecting networks lets information flow, and that also **opens security risks** **[L]**.

> 💭 *"Why do we need firewalls?"*

A **firewall** controls network traffic using **configured security rules**. It can block unauthorized connections and allow traffic that meets the rules **[L]**. Its purpose is to help protect a network from unwanted or harmful traffic **[L]**.

An extra Cisco-community definition adds one useful word: a firewall acts like a **filter as traffic moves from one network to another**, and its policies are usually defined by network administrators **[S3]**.

*Analogy (mine, not sourced): a security guard at the door, checking each visitor against a list of rules.*

A company might, for example, restrict unauthorized connections to its internal systems *(your example)*.

## 📶 Cutting the Cable: Wireless

Not every device can plug into a cable. Phones and laptops often connect wirelessly through a **wireless access point (AP)** **[L]**.

The AP lets those devices communicate with the network using wireless signals **[L]**. In other words, it connects wireless clients to the wired network **[S2]**.

### 📦 The Box on Your Shelf

At home, you probably never see a separate switch, router, firewall and AP. Your lecture explains why: **many home networking devices combine routing, switching, wireless access and firewall functions in one physical unit** **[L]**. An independent Cisco training article says the same, describing a home box that can be a cable/DSL modem, router, firewall, switch and wireless access point all at once **[S1]**.

In larger organizations, those jobs are often handled by **separate devices**, because bigger networks need more capacity and more specialized features **[L]**.

```

COMPANY:  [Firewall]──[Router]──[Switch]──[Access Point]
              each doing one job, at larger scale
```

## 🚪 Where Does the Outside World Come In?

> 💭 *"My home network is connected, but how does it reach the Internet?"*

**Heads-up:** your lecture notes do not mention the modem, the ISP, or LAN vs WAN. This section and the next come from your own notes, and I verified them against outside sources.

A **modem** connects your computer or network to your **Internet Service Provider (ISP)** so you can get online **[S7]**. A router then sits between the modem and your network, directing traffic to the right destination **[S7]**. Many ISPs hand out a **gateway**, a single device combining modem and router functions **[S7]**.

## 🏠 Small World, Big World: LAN and WAN

Two words you will hear constantly:

| | **LAN** 🏠 | **WAN** 🌍 |
|---|---|---|
| **Full name** | Local Area Network | Wide Area Network |
| **Reach** | A limited area such as a home, office, school or campus [S4] | A large geographic area, usually connecting multiple LANs [S5] |
| **Your notes** | Switches connect devices inside it | Routers forward traffic between networks |
| **Example** | Your home Wi-Fi | The Internet itself is considered a WAN [S5] |

One nuance worth keeping: Cloudflare says a LAN is usually within a single building. If it spans several buildings, it may be more accurate to call it a MAN or WAN **[S4]**. A big college campus can therefore be described differently depending on who you ask.

And the Internet? It is a **network of networks** **[S4]**, which matches your lecture's idea of networks connecting networks globally **[L]**.

## 📬 The Journey of One Request

> 💭 *"How does data actually travel across the Internet?"*

Imagine you open an AI application in your browser. Your notes sketch the trip like this:

```
Your Laptop → Wi-Fi AP / Router → ISP Network → Internet → Application Server
```

Step by step:

1. 💻 Your device prepares the data to send.
2. 📦 The data is divided into **packets** where the relevant protocols require it.
3. 🛣️ Routers forward those packets toward the destination.
4. 🖥️ The server receives and processes the request.
5. ↩️ A response travels back to your device.

> ⚠️ **Important correction to the "chain" picture:** your request is not one physical object riding a fixed chain of devices. Packets travel independently and can take different routes if necessary **[S8]**. Protocols and routing decisions determine the exact path **[S6][S8]**.

## 🧑‍💻 Who Keeps All This Running?

Behind every working network are **network engineers**. They design, configure, maintain and troubleshoot networks so devices and systems can communicate reliably **[L]**.

Your course introduces the **CCNA (Cisco Certified Network Associate)**, a certification covering networking technologies and concepts **[L]**. The course will explore switches, routers, firewalls, wireless access points, servers and other networking components **[L]**.

For reference, the current CCNA exam code is **200-301**, a **120-minute** exam covering network fundamentals, network access, IP connectivity, IP services, security fundamentals, and automation and programmability **[S9]**. Exam details can change, so check Cisco before registering.

## 🌀 Deepest Realization

You never really use "the Internet". You use **a stack of small, boring devices**, each solving one limitation of the one before it:

- Cable limits → **switch**
- Network-to-network limits → **router**
- Safety limits → **firewall**
- Wire limits → **access point**

That is exactly the sequence your instructor used: **Individual computers → Switches connecting devices → Routers connecting networks → The Internet connecting networks globally** **[L]**. Each device exists because the previous idea hit a wall.
